## Sample 1 — The changing role of brands

Brands are having to change the role they play to even be seen.

Some of the most interesting marketing at the moment is coming from brands you might once have described as established and traditional, and I've worked for a few of them.

They’re having to find new customers, new reasons to be noticed and new ways to stay culturally relevant. Brands are stretching well beyond their traditional proposition through collaborations, culture, communities and fandoms.

Something that came up in a conversation last week has stayed with me: the shift from building an audience to enabling connections between the people within it.

That's increasingly important.

People don't necessarily want to join a community because of a brand. They connect around shared interests, passions and experiences, and the brand creates the space, reason or opportunity for those connections to happen. That changes the dynamic.

The brand isn't at the heart of every interaction; it’s the facilitator of something people want to be part of.

Perhaps that’s part of why we’re seeing established brands stretch into culture, fandom and experience. It creates more ways for people to connect with the brand, while allowing the relationship to grow into something more meaningful than simply being a customer.
The challenge is making sure the stretch still feels authentic, rather than simply chasing whatever happens to be culturally hot at the time.

The role of the brand is changing. The interesting question is how far it can stretch while still feeling unmistakably itself.

## Sample 2 — English and Welsh wine, and the lag

English and Welsh wine has been hard to miss in the mainstream media recently.

Strong harvests, increasing production, growing recognition and a broader range of wines. Wine has been grown here for decades, but the latest generation has built a very different level of reputation and visibility. Sparkling has played a huge part in that, but still wines are part of the picture too.

The 2025 harvest produced around 16.5 million bottles, making it the UK’s second-largest harvest, and 2026 is looking promising again.

But what interests me most isn’t simply the growth. It’s the lag.

A vineyard planted today won't give you a product to sell next year. Vines need time to mature and, depending on the wine being made, it may be several more years before the finished bottle reaches the market.

Some of what consumers are seeing today reflects bold decisions made six, eight or even ten years ago.

That’s where my marketing brain kicks in.

A lot can happen to a customer in eight years. Tastes change, spending changes, competitors change, new occasions emerge, the meaning of premium shifts, and other businesses spot the same customer opportunity.

Wine is not unique.  Many businesses have to make long-term decisions based on a view of the opportunity, the market and the customer at a particular moment. Then they invest in product, capacity, experiences, technology, infrastructure or brand.

The challenge is that, while the business is building, the customer keeps moving.

Once you’ve made the long-term decision, you have to live with it. What you can change is how you respond. The danger is continuing to operate on assumptions that were right when the decision was made, but may no longer be right. You have to keep understanding how the customer is changing to be successful.

I’m finding that particularly relevant as I build a brand of my own. It is making me think not only about staying close to the customer, but about speed: what genuinely needs time, what can be tested earlier and how quickly an idea can get in front of real people.

For me, that’s the commercial lesson in the wine story.

The longer the journey from decision to customer, the more important it is to shorten the feedback loop.

## Sample 3 — "It's the market"

My recent post about estate agents led to quite a few conversations.

I’ve spoken with friends, people going through similar moves, and plenty of others who simply have an opinion on what’s happening. And there’s one phrase I keep hearing:
“It’s the market.”

It's a familiar phrase in the housing market, but I'm hearing versions of it across lots of businesses at the moment too.

Customers are harder to find. Decisions are taking longer. People are more cautious and more selective.  So yes, perhaps it is the market.  But what does that actually mean?

Are there genuinely fewer buyers? Are they simply waiting? Have priorities changed? Are they looking somewhere else? Is it the looming Budget, the cost of living, confidence, interest rates?

Perhaps.  But everything is an assumption until you know.

That matters, because acting too quickly on assumptions can create another problem.

If you change the price, the offer or the proposition before understanding what is really holding people back, you risk making the wrong decision about your value.

Sometimes holding your nerve long enough to understand the problem matters just as much as acting. That means staying open-minded.

Ask the customers. Talk to the people who looked but didn’t buy. Find out what has changed, what is holding them back and what would make them act today rather than tomorrow.

Because “it’s the market” may be true.  But it isn’t enough to tell you what to do next.

In a tougher market, clarity becomes more valuable, not less.

Not clarity based on assumptions, but clarity built from understanding what is actually happening, and the creativity to find new ways of reaching and speaking to customers.

No one can manufacture demand that isn’t there. But you can work much harder to find where it does exist, understand what matters to those customers, and focus your effort there.

Sometimes growth isn’t about shouting louder — or changing things faster. It starts with understanding what “it’s the market” really means.
